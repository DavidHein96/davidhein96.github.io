The Problem That Started It All

A researcher walks into your office with 2,000 pathology reports and a spreadsheet with 15 empty columns. Tumor subtype. Histologic grade. Margin status. Biomarker results. They need it filled in by next month. The traditional approach? Hire a team of trained abstractors to read each report and manually enter the data. For 2,000 reports with multiple fields each, that’s roughly 200-300 person-hours of tedious, error-prone work — at a cost of thousands of dollars and weeks of calendar time.

This is the bottleneck that launched the project. We wanted to know: can an LLM read pathology reports and extract structured data as accurately as a trained human?

The answer, after six months of development, is yes — but only if you’re systematic about understanding how the LLM fails and why. That process of systematic failure analysis became the intellectual core of the paper, published in npj Digital Medicine.

The Approach

Why Not LangChain?

Early on, we made a deliberate architectural choice: no frameworks. We built the pipeline from composable primitives — Jinja2 for prompt templates, Pydantic for output validation, and Microsoft Prompt Flow for orchestration. The reasoning was practical: LangChain and similar frameworks add abstraction layers that make debugging harder, and when you’re trying to understand why a model misclassified a chromophobe renal cell carcinoma as clear cell, the last thing you want is to trace through three layers of chain abstraction. Jinja2 templates are just text files you can read, edit, and version-control. Pydantic models are just Python dataclasses with validation. When something breaks, you can see exactly what prompt was sent and what came back.

The pipeline uses these modular Jinja2 prompt templates across three extraction flow types depending on report structure:

  • Report-level extraction — single label per document (e.g., tumor subtype)
  • Specimen-level extraction — one label per specimen in multi-specimen reports
  • Panel-level extraction — structured results from IHC/FISH panels with specimen details

Each flow follows a two-stage process: segmentation of the report into relevant sections, then standardization of extracted values into structured JSON output validated by Pydantic models. Here’s a simplified example of what a Jinja2 prompt template looks like:

# Simplified Jinja2 template for tumor subtype extraction
template = """
You are a pathology data extraction assistant.

Given the following pathology report, extract the kidney tumor subtype.

REPORT:
{{ report_text }}

Respond with ONLY a JSON object matching this schema:
{
  "tumor_subtype": one of {{ allowed_subtypes | join(', ') }},
  "confidence": "high" or "low",
  "evidence_quote": "relevant text from the report"
}
"""

And the corresponding Pydantic model that validates every response:

from pydantic import BaseModel, field_validator
from typing import Literal

class TumorSubtypeResult(BaseModel):
    tumor_subtype: str
    confidence: Literal["high", "low"]
    evidence_quote: str

    @field_validator("tumor_subtype")
    @classmethod
    def validate_subtype(cls, v):
        allowed = {"clear_cell", "papillary", "chromophobe",
                   "ccprct", "tfe3_rearranged", "tfeb_altered"}
        if v.lower() not in allowed:
            raise ValueError(f"Unknown subtype: {v}")
        return v.lower()

This pattern — template renders prompt, LLM returns JSON, Pydantic validates structure — repeats across every extraction task. When validation fails, the error message tells you exactly what went wrong.

The Error Ontology: Learning to Categorize Failure

The error ontology emerged from a moment of frustration. After our first pass at prompt engineering, we had a pile of mismatches between LLM output and our gold-standard annotations — but no systematic way to understand what kind of mismatches they were. Some were the LLM being wrong. Some were our gold-standard annotations being wrong (pathologists disagree more often than you’d think). Some were neither the LLM nor the human being wrong, but our extraction schema being ambiguous.

So we built a taxonomy. Every single discrepancy gets classified by:

  • Source: LLM error, manual annotation error, or schema/instruction gap
  • Severity: Major (clinically significant) vs. minor
  • Context: Where in the report the error originated

Here’s a concrete example of why this matters. In one early iteration, the model consistently extracted “clear cell renal cell carcinoma” from reports that actually described clear cell papillary renal cell tumor (CCPRCT) — a completely different entity with a different prognosis and treatment pathway. The reports often contained phrases like “clear cell morphology with papillary architecture,” and the model latched onto “clear cell” and ignored the qualifying context. That’s a major LLM error — clinically significant, because misclassifying CCPRCT as clear cell RCC could lead to unnecessary aggressive treatment.

The fix wasn’t just “add CCPRCT to the allowed values.” We had to restructure the prompt to explicitly ask the model to distinguish between clear cell subtypes, provide example disambiguation cases, and add a confidence flag that triggers human review for borderline cases.

Six Iterations: From Naive to Near-Perfect

The development arc tells a story:

  1. Iteration 1: Naive prompts, no output validation. ~15% major error rate. Most errors were the LLM hallucinating subtypes or misinterpreting multi-specimen reports.
  2. Iterations 2-3: Added Pydantic validation and report segmentation. Major errors dropped to ~8%. Discovered that many “LLM errors” were actually annotation errors in our gold standard.
  3. Iterations 4-5: Refined the error ontology, corrected gold-standard labels, added disambiguation examples to prompts. Major errors below 3%.
  4. Iteration 6: Final tuning of edge cases — rare subtypes, ambiguous pathologist language, multi-tumor specimens. Major LLM errors: 0.99% (14 out of 1,413 entities).

The key insight was that the error ontology didn’t just help us fix the LLM — it helped us fix our own assumptions. Roughly 30% of the discrepancies we initially attributed to the model turned out to be errors in the human-annotated gold standard. The iterative process improved both sides.

Key Results

What This Saves in Practice

To put the results in practical terms: extracting structured data from 2,297 pathology reports would typically require a trained abstractor working full-time for 2-4 weeks, depending on the complexity of the extraction schema. Our pipeline processes the same volume in under 2 hours of compute time, producing a validated spreadsheet that requires only spot-check review. That’s not just faster — it means a researcher can go from “I have an idea for a study” to “I have analysis-ready data” in a day instead of a month.

We validated the finalized pipeline on 2,297 internal kidney tumor reports with pre-existing templated data:

Task Macro-Averaged F1
Kidney tumor subtype classification (6 subtypes) 0.99
Clear cell RCC 0.99
Papillary RCC 0.99
Chromophobe RCC 0.99
CCPRCT 0.98
TFE3-rearranged RCC 1.0
TFEB-altered RCC 1.0
Metastatic kidney cancer detection 0.97

The pipeline also demonstrated cross-domain adaptability: applied to publicly available TCGA breast cancer reports (89% agreement) and prostate cancer Gleason scoring (98% agreement) without domain-specific retraining.

What’s Next

The pipeline is already being extended to new domains — we’re actively applying it to extract treatment timelines from longitudinal EHR notes (not just single pathology reports), which introduces temporal reasoning and cross-document coreference challenges that are significantly harder than single-document extraction. The error ontology framework is proving even more valuable in these complex settings, where the failure modes are subtler and more varied.

An open question I keep coming back to: When should you trust the model’s output without human review? Our 0.99% major error rate sounds impressive, but in a dataset of 10,000 reports, that’s still ~100 clinically significant errors. For research purposes, that’s often acceptable. For clinical decision-making, it’s not. The gap between “good enough for research” and “good enough for the clinic” is where the hardest problems live.
